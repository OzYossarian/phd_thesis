\section{Focked up Pauli webs}\label{sec:focked-up-pauli-webs}

We start by generalising the definition of Pauli webs from qubits to qumodes.
In a nutshell, everything is exactly the same, except we replace $\mathbb{F}_2$ with $\mathbb{R}$ throughout, and we replace Paulis $X^a$ and $Z^b$ with displacements $\hat{X}(a) = \hat{D}(\binom{a}{0})$ and $\hat{Z}(b) = \hat{D}(\binom{0}{b})$.
For instance, we start in the same way, by defining a highlighting.
Recall for a diagram $D$ with edges $E = E_\circ \cup E_{\boundary}$ partitioned into internal and boundary edges, we denoted the half-edges as $E_{1/2} = E_\circ \sqcup E_\circ \cup E_{\boundary}$.

\begin{definition}
    A highlighting $H$ on a Focked up ZX diagram $D$ with edges $E$ is an element $H = \tbinom{\bsym{a}}{\bsym{b}}$ of $\RR^{2|E_{1/2}|}$.
    Graphically, we highlight each half-edge $e_j$ in $D$ red and green and label it $(a_j, b_j)$.
    If either of $a_j$ or $b_j$ are zero we can omit the red or green highlight, respectively.
\end{definition}

We redeploy our usual example of a single internal edge within a general diagram $D$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:highlighting_general_diagram}
\tikzfig{qumodes/focked_up/pauli_webs/highlighting_general_diagram}
\end{equation}

We can generalise the notion of firing generators in a diagram according to a highlighting (\Cref{eq:firing_generators_example_qubit}).
We again choose the convention that red $\hat{X}$ displacements are the `inner' gates and green $\hat{Z}$ displacements are the `outer' ones:

    ~~\mapsto~~

\end{equation}
We use the same notation as before: for a diagram $D$ with generators $V$ and a highlighting $H$, we write the diagram with all generators fired according to $H$ as $D^H$.
If only firing a subset of generators $V' \subseteq V$, we write the resulting diagram as $D^{H|_{V'}}$.

To define Pauli webs, we must start with single generators.
We're considering the generators of the diagram to be the three spider species, the multiplier, the identity map and the swap map.
Recall we don't allow spiderless cups and caps.

\begin{definition}\label{defn:pauli_web_generator}
    A \textbf{Pauli web} $W$ on a generator $f$ with edges $E$ is a highlighting $W \in \RR^{2|E_{1/2}|}$ such that the result $f^W$ of firing $f$ according to $W$ is proportional to $f$ again: $f^W \propto f$.
    Graphically, it's a highlighting:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:pauli_web_generator_highlighting}
        \tikzfig{qumodes/focked_up/pauli_webs/highlighting_generator}
    \end{equation}
    satisfying:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:pauli_web_generator_condition}
        \tikzfig{qumodes/focked_up/pauli_webs/pauli_web_generator_condition}
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

This then lets us define Pauli webs on general diagrams.

\begin{definition}\label{defn:pauli_web_general_diagram}
    A \textbf{Pauli web} $W$ on a general ZX-diagram $D$ with edges $E$ is a highlighting $W \in \RR^{2|E_{1/2}|}$ such that the restriction $W|_f$ to each generator $f$ is a Pauli web on $f$, and for every pair of half-edges in an internal edge the labels $(a, b)$ and $(c, d)$ satisfy $c = -a$ and $d = -b$.
\end{definition}
Graphically, on an internal edge of a general diagram, we have:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pauli_web_general_diagram_conditions}
    \tikzfig{qumodes/focked_up/pauli_webs/pauli_web_general_diagram}
\end{equation}

Given diagram $D$ with edges $E$, in the qubit case, the condition that half-edges making up an internal edge had to have the same label in $W$ meant we could freely consider a Pauli web $W \in \FF_2^{2|E_{1/2}|}$ to in fact be an element of $\FF_2^{|E|}$.
For qumodes, we don't immediately have this freedom, because the half-edge labels need only be negations of one another.
But since we've forbidden spiderless cups and caps, every internal edge is the output of one generator and the input of another.
So we can define the convention that labelling an internal edge $(a, b)$ means the output half-edge has the label $(a, b)$, and the input half-edge has the label $(-a, -b)$.
\begin{equation}
    \tikzfig{qumodes/focked_up/pauli_webs/highlighting_generator_label_convention}
\end{equation}

By definition the set of all highlightings on a diagram $D$ with edges $E$ form a group.
One can again show that the set of all Pauli webs on $D$ is a subgroup of this via the subgroup test, and will as before denote this group as $\WWW(D)$.

\subsection{Pauli webs on Gaussian + comb diagrams}

From now on, we'll focus on the Gaussian + comb fragment only.
First, single Gaussian spiders.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:general_pauli_web_green_gaussian_spider}
A highlighting of a Gaussian $Z$ spider with phase function $e^{i2\pi(t + u\isarg{x} + v\isarg{x}^2)}$ is a Pauli web iff it's of the following form, where $\sum_j a_j = -2vb$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:general_pauli_web_green_gaussian_spider}
    \tikzfig{qumodes/focked_up/pauli_webs/general_pauli_web_green_gaussian_spider}
\end{equation}
\begin{proof}
    Start with a generic highlighting on a Gaussian spider, and fire it to get:
    \begin{equation}
        \tikzfig{qumodes/focked_up/pauli_webs/general_pauli_web_green_gaussian_spider_fired}
    \end{equation}
    Now copy the red $\chi_{b_1}$ displacement through the Gaussian spider, commute the green displacements through the red ones, and fuse them into the central spider:
    \begingroup
    \allowdisplaybreaks
    \begin{align}
        &\phantom{~~=~~} \tikzfig{qumodes/focked_up/pauli_webs/general_pauli_web_green_gaussian_spider_proof_1} \\[20pt]
        &~~=~~ \tikzfig{qumodes/focked_up/pauli_webs/general_pauli_web_green_gaussian_spider_proof_2} \\[20pt]
        &~~=~~ \tikzfig{qumodes/focked_up/pauli_webs/general_pauli_web_green_gaussian_spider_proof_3} \\[20pt]
        &~~=~~ \tikzfig{qumodes/focked_up/pauli_webs/general_pauli_web_green_gaussian_spider_proof_4}
    \end{align}
    \endgroup
    Here we've defined $a$ and $\lambda$ to be
    \begin{equation}
        a = \sum_{j=1}^{l+m} a_j
        \qqquad
        \lambda = e^{i2\pi(\sum_{j=2}^{l} a_j (b_j - b_1) + \sum_{j=l+1}^{l+m} a_j(b_j + b_1))}
    \end{equation}
    This is proportional to the original Gaussian spider iff all the red spiders disappear and the new function in the central spider is equal the original Gaussian function.
    The former means $b_2 = \ldots = b_l = b_1$ and $b_{l+1} = \ldots = b_{l+m} = -b_1$.
    Note that then $\lambda = 1$.
    Defining $b \coloneqq b_1$, the latter means:
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{aligned}
            e^{i2\pi(t + u\isarg{x} + v\isarg{x}^2)}
            &= e^{i2\pi(t + (u + 2vb)\isarg{x} + v\isarg{x}^2)}\overline{\chi}_a \\
            &= e^{i2\pi(t + (u + 2vb)\isarg{x} + v\isarg{x}^2)}e^{i2\pi a\isarg{x}} \\
            &= e^{i2\pi(t + (u + 2vb + a)\isarg{x} + v\isarg{x}^2)} \\
        \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
    This occurs iff $2vb + a = 0$.
\end{proof}
\end{proposition}

Next, single comb spiders.
We'll in fact restrict our attention to comb spiders with label $\indicator{x \eqmod{r} c}$ where $\frac{c}{r - c}$ is rational, assuming without loss of generality that $0 \leq c < r$.
This therefore includes the cases $\indicator{x \eqmod{\sqrt{2}} 0}$ and $\indicator{x \eqmod{\sqrt{2}} 1/\sqrt{2}}$ that are relevant for the square GKP code.
We'll start with separate lemmas about symmetries of the function $\indicator{x \eqmod{r} c}$, for certain $c$.
We consider the cases $c = 0$ and $0 < c < r$ separately.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:comb_function_symmetries_0}
    Consider the function $\indicator{x \eqmod{r} 0}$.
    We have $\indicator{x \eqmod{r} - b} \cdot \overline{\chi}_a(x) = \indicator{x \eqmod{r} 0}$ iff $b \in r\mathbb{Z}$ and $a \in \frac{1}{r}\mathbb{Z}$.
    \begin{proof}
        This is best done geometrically, I reckon.
        Picture the function $f = \indicator{x \eqmod{r} 0}$, to start with; this is just the line $f(x) = 0$ but with discontinuities at all points in $r\mathbb{Z}$, where instead $f(x) = 1$.
        So we can imagine dotted lines between each pair of points $(kr, 0)$ and $(kr, 1)$, which will form teeth of a comb.
        It will also be helpful to imagine a dotted line $f(x) = 1$:
        \begin{equation}
            \includegraphics[valign=c, width=.8\linewidth]{figures/qumodes/focked_up/pauli_webs/indicator_comb}
        \end{equation}
        The effect of transforming the function to $\indicator{x \eqmod{r} - b}$ is to shift it leftwards by $b$, and the effect of multiplying this by $\overline{\chi}_a(x) = e^{i2\pi ax}$ is to twist the whole function around the real axis with a period of $1/a$.
        For example, setting $b = 0$ and $1/a = r$:
        \begin{equation}
            \includegraphics[valign=c, width=.8\linewidth]{figures/qumodes/focked_up/pauli_webs/indicator_comb_twisted}
        \end{equation}
        From this, one can hopefully already see that in order for this new function to equal the original the shift by $b$ must be of a multiple of $r$, i.e.\ $b \in r\mathbb{Z}$.
        Assuming this is the case, the question is then when $\indicator{x \eqmod{r} 0} \cdot \overline{\chi}_a(x) = \indicator{x \eqmod{r} 0}$.
        We can see that twisting by $\overline{\chi}_a(x) = e^{i2\pi ax}$ leaves the comb invariant iff the twist period $\frac{1}{a}$ is an integer divisor of $r$.
        That is, iff there's an integer $k$ such that $k\frac{1}{a} = r$, or equivalently iff $a \in \frac{1}{r}\mathbb{Z}$.
    \end{proof}
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:comb_function_symmetries_c}
    Consider the function $\indicator{x \eqmod{r} c}$ where $c > 0$.
    Further assume $\frac{c}{r - c}$ is rational, and without loss of generality $0 < c < r$.
    We can write $\frac{c}{r - c} = \frac{u}{v}$ for fully simplified $\frac{u}{v}$ (i.e.\ $u$ and $v$ are coprime).
    We have $\indicator{x \eqmod{r} c - b} \cdot \overline{\chi}_a(x) = \indicator{x \eqmod{r} c}$ iff $b \in r\mathbb{Z}$ and either $a \in \frac{u}{c}\mathbb{Z}$ if $c \leq r - c$ else $a \in \frac{v}{r - c}\mathbb{Z}$ if $c > r - c$.
    \begin{proof}
        The first part of the proof is exactly the same as the previous lemma, and we again find the shift by $b$ needs to be a multiple of $r$.
        Supposing this is the case, the question is then when $\indicator{x \eqmod{r} c} \cdot \overline{\chi}_a(x) = \indicator{x \eqmod{r} c}$.
        Assume that $c \leq r - c$; if not, consider instead $\frac{r - c}{c} = \frac{v}{u}$ in what follows.
        We can write both $c$ and $r - c$ as integer multiples of $\frac{c}{u}$:
        \begin{equation}
            c = u \cdot \frac{c}{u}
            \qqquad
            r - c = v \cdot \frac{c}{u}
        \end{equation}
        Since $\frac{u}{v}$ is fully simplified, $\frac{c}{u}$ is in fact the largest common integer divisor of $c$ and $r - c$.
        We can therefore see that twisting by $\overline{\chi}_a(x) = e^{i2\pi ax}$ leaves the comb invariant iff the twist period $\frac{1}{a}$ is an integer divisor of $\frac{c}{u}$.
        That is, iff $a \in \frac{u}{c}\mathbb{Z}$.
        (If instead $r - c < c$, we end up with the iff condition that $\frac{1}{a}$ must divide $\frac{r - c}{v}$, hence $a \in \frac{v}{r - c}\mathbb{Z}$).
    \end{proof}
\end{lemma}

Since the second lemma in particular is a bit of a mouthful, we give the cases of the square GKP combs concretely.

\begin{example}
    Consider the function $\indicator{x \eqmod{\sqrt{2}} 0}$.
    We have $\indicator{x \eqmod{\sqrt{2}} - b} \cdot \overline{\chi}_a(x) = \indicator{x \eqmod{\sqrt{2}} 0}$ iff $b \in \sqrt{2}\mathbb{Z}$ and $a \in \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathbb{Z}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
    Consider the function $\indicator{x \eqmod{\sqrt{2}} \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}}$.
    So setting $r = \sqrt{2}$ and $c = \sqrt{2}/2$, we have $c \leq r - c$ and $c/r-c = 1/2 \eqqcolon u/v$.
    We get $\indicator{x \eqmod{\sqrt{2}} \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} - b} \cdot \overline{\chi}_a(x) = \indicator{x \eqmod{\sqrt{2}} \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}}$ iff $a, b \in \sqrt{2}\mathbb{Z}$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Classifying Pauli webs}

As in the qubit case, we can classify webs on a diagram $D$ into four categories: detecting webs, (co)stabilizing webs, and logical webs.
Detecting webs are those that highlight no input or output edges of the diagram, and form a group $\WWW_\DDD(D)$.
Stabilizing webs are those that highlight no input edges and form a group $\WWW_\SSS(D)$ that contains $\WWW_\DDD(D)$ as a subgroup.
Dually, costabilizing webs are those that highlight output edges and form a group $\WWW^\SSS(D)$ that contains $\WWW_\DDD(D)$ as a subgroup.
We again let $\WWW_\SSS^\SSS(D)$ denote the group generated by all stabilizing and costabilizing webs.
The group of logical webs is then the quotient group $\WWW(D)/\WWW_\SSS^\SSS(D)$, with (non-trivial) logical webs being elements of (non-trivial) cosets, or perhaps sometimes cosets themselves.
As in the qubit case, one can surely show group isomorphisms connecting Pauli webs to the CV stabilizer formalism -- I have run out of time to do this, so leave it as a loose end of this thesis, to be tied up later, perhaps.

\subsection{Detecting webs detect errors}

What we will look at is exactly which errors detecting webs can detect.
We'll discuss error models a bit more carefully in the next section, but for now let's just say, for a diagram $D$ with edges $E$, errors are displacements $e^{i\theta}D(\binom{\bsym{q}}{\bsym{p}}) \in \hat{\DDD}_{|E|}$.
We use the same notation as before, with $D^F$ denoting the diagram $D$ with the error $F$ applied to the corresponding edges.
Error $F$ is \textit{trivial} iff $D^F \propto D$, and errors $F$ and $F'$ are \textit{equivalent} iff $D^F = D^{F'}$.
In the qubit case, because of the link we established between webs and the stabilizer formalism, we could truly show that detectable errors changed products of measurement outcomes that were otherwise deterministic in the absence of errors.
Since we haven't established such a link here, we'll take a shortcut and just \textit{define} detectable errors appropriately.

\begin{definition}\label{defn:detectable_error}
    An error $F$ on diagram $D$ is detectable iff it's non-trivial and $D^F$ is the zero linear map.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:detecting_web_detects_error}
    Suppose we have a diagram $D$ with edges $E$ and detecting web $W = \binom{\bsym{a}}{\bsym{b}}$.
    A non-trivial error $F = \hat{D}(\binom{\bsym{q}}{\bsym{p}})$ is detectable iff $\binom{\bsym{a}}{\bsym{b}} \odot \binom{\bsym{q}}{\bsym{p}} \notin \ZZ$, or equivalently iff the displacement $\hat{D}(\binom{\bsym{a}}{\bsym{b}})$ doesn't commute with $F = \hat{D}(\binom{\bsym{q}}{\bsym{p}})$.
    \begin{proof}
        First recall that any diagram that's equal to a scalar multiple $\lambda$ of itself other than $\lambda = 1$ is necessarily zero:
        \begin{equation}
            \tikzfig{qumodes/focked_up/pauli_webs/diagram_equal_to_non_1_multiple_of_itself_is_zero}
        \end{equation}
        So now let's take a close look at a single internal edge $j$ of the diagram $D^F$ described above.
        We can fire the Pauli web $W$, and commute the resulting displacement $\hat{D}(\binom{a_j}{b_j})$ past the displacement $\hat{D}(\binom{q_j}{p_j})$ from the error $F$, at the cost of introducing a global phase (\Cref{lem:displacement_commutation_relation_zx}).
        We can then cancel out $\hat{D}(\binom{a_j}{b_j})$ thanks to the $\hat{D}(\binom{-a_j}{-b_j})$, leaving just the original displacement operator $\hat{D}(\binom{q_j}{p_j})$ on this edge:
        \begin{equation}
            \tikzfig{qumodes/focked_up/pauli_webs/pauli_web_general_diagram_internal_fault_web_fired}
        \end{equation}
        We can repeat this process across all edges, picking up a total phase of
        \begin{equation}
            \lambda = e^{i2\pi \sum_{j = 1}^{|E|} a_j p_j - b_j q_j} = e^{i2\pi \binom{\bsym{a}}{\bsym{b}} \odot \binom{\bsym{q}}{\bsym{p}}}
        \end{equation}
        Since a detecting web by definition has $a_j = b_j = 0$ on any boundary edges, after performing this process for all edges we get back the original diagram but multiplied by this phase $\lambda$.
        So if $\lambda$ isn't $1$, the diagram $D^F$ is zero, meaning $F$ is detectable.
        This occurs iff $\binom{\bsym{a}}{\bsym{b}} \odot \binom{\bsym{q}}{\bsym{p}} \notin \ZZ$.
    \end{proof}
\end{proposition}

\subsection{The ZX$/\chi$ calculus}\label{subsec:zx_mod_chi}

In \Cref{subsec:zx_mod_pi} in the qubit half of this thesis, we showed that Pauli webs on a diagram were unchanged (up to global phases) when spider labels were defined mod $\pi$ instead of mod $2\pi$.
Since Pauli webs essentially determine all error correction properties, we defined the $\zxmodpi$-calculus in which labels are defined mod $\pi$.
Thus, in this quotient calculus, equivalent representatives of diagrams have fundamentally the same error correction properties.
We have an analogous result in the qumode case, because multiplying a green or red spider label by $\chi_a$ for any $a \in \RR$ doesn't change the Pauli webs on that spider (up to global phase).

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:web_invariant_mod_chi}
    Suppose a green or red spider with at least one leg has label $f$ (if it has no legs, then it can have no Pauli webs, so isn't relevant to this discussion).
    Let $W = \tbinom{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}$ be a Pauli web on this spider.
    This highlighting is still a Pauli web of this spider if its label is changed to $f \cdot \chi_c$, for any $c \in \RR$.
    \begin{proof}
        We'll use a green spider in this proof -- the proof for the red spider is identical.
        Fire the spider according to the web, then unfuse the $\chi_c$ part onto any one of the legs.
        This will commute (up to global phase) past the displacements coming from firing the spider (\Cref{lem:displacement_commutation_relation_zx}).
        \begin{equation}
            \begin{aligned}
                &\phantom{~~=~~}
                \tikzfig{qumodes/focked_up/pauli_webs/highlighting_green_spider_f_chi} \\[10pt]
                &~~\mapsto~~
                \tikzfig{qumodes/focked_up/pauli_webs/highlighting_green_spider_f_chi_fired} \\[10pt]
                &~~=~~
                \tikzfig{qumodes/focked_up/pauli_webs/highlighting_green_spider_f_chi_fired_unfused} \\[10pt]
                &~~\propto~~
                \tikzfig{qumodes/focked_up/pauli_webs/highlighting_green_spider_f_chi_fired_unfused_commuted}
            \end{aligned}
        \end{equation}
        Now use the fact that $W$ is a Pauli web for the spider labelled $f$:
        \begin{equation}
            \begin{aligned}
                &\phantom{~~=~~}
                \tikzfig{qumodes/focked_up/pauli_webs/highlighting_green_spider_f_chi_fired_unfused_commuted} \\[10pt]
                &~~\propto~~
                \tikzfig{qumodes/focked_up/pauli_webs/highlighting_green_spider_f_chi_fired_unfused_commuted_fused} \\[10pt]
                &~~=~~
                \tikzfig{qumodes/focked_up/pauli_webs/highlighting_green_spider_f_chi_fired_unfused_commuted_fused_2}
            \end{aligned}
        \end{equation}
    \end{proof}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:web_invariant_mod_chi}
    $W$ is a Pauli web for a green or red spider labelled $f$ iff it's a web for the same spider labelled $f \cdot \chi_c$ for any $c \in \RR$.
    \begin{proof}
        The lemma above proves both direction, because $f = (f \cdot \chi_c) \cdot \chi_{-c}$.
    \end{proof}
\end{proposition}

Therefore we can define a ZX-calculus mod $\chi$.

\begin{definition}\label{defn:zx_mod_chi}
    The $\zxmodchi$-calculus is the quotient calculus formed by the equivalence relation $D \sim_\chi D'$ iff $D$ and $D'$ are equal when green and red spider labels are defined mod $\chi_c$ for any $c \in \RR$.
    Equivalently, it's the quotient calculus defined by adding the following extra rewrite rule:
    \begin{equation}
        \tikzfig{qumodes/focked_up/pauli_webs/gate_green_chi}
        \quad = \quad
        \tikzfig{qumodes/focked_up/pauli_webs/id}
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}
